## 1\. The one idea behind everything

People don't speak in sentences. They speak in **attempts**. Listen to any real conversation and nobody delivers a finished thought. They start, back up, grab a better word, laugh, get interrupted, and circle back. In the two original conversations you hear it constantly: *"it's a, it's an episode about us."* *"I don't even know if I can get through the opening of this."*

That mess isn't a flaw in the talk. **The mess is the talk.** It's thinking happening out loud, and it's exactly where personality and feeling leak through.

Written dialogue goes wrong when it's too *finished*: complete grammar, perfect clarity, tidy emotional statements. Readers can't name what's off, but they feel a script instead of a person.

The goal is not to copy speech. A raw transcript is unreadable (see Appendix A). The goal is to **suggest** speech. Treat real talk as source material and your dialogue as the sculpture: keep roughly ten percent of the mess, and choose which ten percent very carefully.

> \*\*THE RULE TO REMEMBER\*\*
> Dialogue isn't what people say. It's what people say while wanting something, hiding something, and only half-listening to each other.

### What "natural" actually means

* **Imperfect, but motivated.** Every stumble has a reason: nerves, thinking, avoiding, or reaching for a word.
* **Specific.** Real people talk about the almond croissant, not "food."
* **Slightly off-target.** People answer the question they wish you'd asked, or the one before it.
* **Emotionally indirect.** The bigger the feeling, the less directly it gets said.
* **Distinct.** You could cover the names and still know who is talking.
* **Shared.** *(new)* People who know each other talk in half-sentences, in-jokes and callbacks. The six new transcripts are almost entirely made of shared history.
* **Situated.** *(new)* Talk happens in a room: a glass, a mic, a plane, a car. Characters react to it, and it interrupts them.

## 2\. Field notes: what real speech actually does

### 2a. From the original two conversations

|Feature|Heard in the transcripts|Why it works in fiction|
|-|-|-|
|**False starts**|"it's a, it's an episode about us" / "I don't even know if I can get through the opening of this"|Shows nerves and live thinking. One restart is enough to make a line feel spoken.|
|**Guessing out loud**|A partner guessing the other's favourite meal, working through countries one by one: Italy, no; England, no; Germany?|Lets the reader watch a mind work. Wrong answers are more alive than right ones.|
|**Answering a neighbouring question**|Asked for a favourite food, one says the first thing that came to mind was bread rolls and butter, then drifts into a childhood stew from home.|Characters follow their own associations. The detour is where the backstory lives.|
|**Searching for the word**|"I don't know the name anymore, the spicy one," "something something spicy."|Nobody has the exact word on demand. Vague words humanise.|
|**Teasing as affection**|"Is that your other boyfriend?" (about a favourite TV show)|Banter is how people who like each other prod each other. Intimacy without a speech.|
|**Talking over each other**|"Who falls asleep first? Three, two, one." "Me." "No, me."|Short, fast, overlapping. Disagreement in single words.|
|**Echoing**|One names a trait; the other repeats it back and turns it into a joke ("you do the personification of considerate").|Repeating the other person's word shows they're actually listening.|
|**A story instead of a summary**|Instead of "I enjoy talking to strangers," the host describes a hotel elevator at 7:30am, seven electrical contractors, and recommending the almond croissant.|A concrete scene does the emotional work for you.|
|**Surprise at one's own feelings**|"I already feel myself getting emotional. I don't know why I'm so moved."|People are often caught off guard by what they feel. They don't announce it like a thesis.|
|**The self-blame reflex**|Told she has changed for the better, she immediately assumes the old her must have been awful.|Real emotion has reflexes that contradict the compliment. Great for complicated characters.|
|**The small confession mid-flow**|In the middle of a lively conversation: "I wish we'd had this conversation two days ago. I sent a text in the heat of an anxiety wave."|The vulnerable line lands inside normal talk, not as a dramatic speech. That's why it hits.|
|**Metaphor from their own life**|Being obsessed with "the curtains on the window" instead of enjoying the view.|Characters reach for images from their world, not generic poetry.|
|**Dumping vs. asking**|She describes going to her husband and unloading the whole problem; he is left wondering what he's supposed to do with it.|Mismatched needs are a goldmine: one person wants to vent, the other wants to fix.|

### 2b. From the six new transcripts *(new)*

|Feature|Heard in the transcripts|Why it works in fiction|
|-|-|-|
|**A running gag that escalates** *(B)*|A comedy-festival night in Montreal starts as a joke about an old meeting, grows into an invented shared backstory ("now we've created this whole thing"), and comes back at the sign-off: "almost as good as Montreal."|One reference, three appearances: setup, escalation, quiet payoff at the exit. It makes two people feel like they have a past.|
|**Undercutting the expected answer** *(C)*|Asked to match a guest's story of being bullied and then lifting weights, the host says his own response to being bullied was to comb his hair into a pompadour.|The grand answer is expected; the small, specific, slightly pathetic one is the character.|
|**A complaint as an extended metaphor** *(C)*|The host, annoyed that a co-host took over the intro, builds a full aviation disaster: "I gave you this mighty craft," then the crash into mountains, the burns, the emergency landing.|Irritation that *plays* is more affectionate than irritation that explains itself.|
|**Interrupting your own tangent** *(A)*|A story about a fast-food job drifts on until a speaker asks, "I'm doing a book on boring stories. Can I use that?" and another says "We got to start."|Someone in the scene is always aware the tangent is a tangent. That awareness is the comedy and the pacing.|
|**Losing the thread, again and again** *(B)*|A speaker keeps stopping: "what was I going to say?" Later he observes that the worst part is forgetting what you were trying to remember.|Forgetting is a character trait. The recovery line, when it arrives, can be the truest thing in the scene.|
|**Deadpan about an object** *(B)*|A mystery drink in a glass: "That's mine. Get your own." Then a straight-faced claim that it gives superhuman strength: "It's why I can lift this glass."|A prop plus total sincerity builds a gag with no setup.|
|**A verbal tic as fingerprint** *(E)*|One speaker keeps saying "Can I defend myself real quick?" before a long, flimsy defence.|A repeated phrase tells the reader who is talking and what they fear: being judged.|
|**Invented jargon** *(E)*|A speaker coins "shadow money" for tip cash he doesn't count. Someone looks it up and finds a real financial term nearby. His reply: "I made it up."|People coin private vocabulary. Use their own words, not industry words.|
|**Collaborative exaggeration, then a deflating fact** *(E)*|One host claims a coworker has six kids and a tattoo from a throwaway remark. The coworker, asked, says "Three."|One person inflates, another adds, and reality arrives in one flat word.|
|**Insult as greeting** *(D)*|A guest walks in and is told he "walks in here like he Bruce Wayne," then is teased about a misspelled name card.|The first lines of a warm scene are often small cuts. They show safety.|
|**Advice wearing a disguise** *(D)*|A friend recalls telling the guest at a premiere, "just don't let them work you to death," then adds he's "allowed to say no." The guest answers with a plain story: seven days without a minute of sleep.|Care often arrives as nagging. The answer is a concrete fact, not a thank-you speech.|
|**Plain disclosure mid-banter** *(D)*|In the middle of a loose conversation, a host says he's in AA. Nearly three years. A friend says, "Thank you, man. Of course." Then the talk moves on.|Short line, short reply, no analysis. The reader feels the weight because nobody makes a speech about it.|
|**The mortality line, then thanks** *(C)*|Near the end, the guest jokes that the host should remind every guest they'll die, then says how much he's enjoying being in the chair.|Jokes about endings let a character say something sincere without owning it.|
|**Warm scripted kindness with an agenda** *(F)*|In a staged pawn-shop scene, a "grieving customer" says her son died and she has no idea what the card is worth. The buyer gets gentler as he gets cheaper.|Sympathy can be a pricing tool. People are warmest when they want something.|
|**Cold reading: questions that carry the answer** *(F)*|A staged "psychic" asks "Did he drive a red car?" and "Were you ever in an accident?" Each question hands the client something to confirm.|One character's questions can do all the talking. The other character is the one who unknowingly writes the scene.|
|**How liars sound** *(F)*|In an impostor game, players complain that one story has "too many details," that it "felt like a Google search," and that a hit-and-run story had an "unnecessary detail" (the dog). The honest player, who turns out to be telling the truth, is dull and a little inconsistent.|Truth is specific but untidy. Lies are either vague or over-decorated.|
|**Patter that covers an action** *(F)*|A magician chats about an Oreo, asks what it says on the packet, "forgot about" a second one, and the trick happens inside the small talk.|Talk is often misdirection. What the character says is not where the action is.|
|**Written voice inside speech** *(all)*|Every episode has a sponsor read: balanced sentences, "here's the deal, and this part matters," a tidy tagline ("the smartest way to hire"). The hosts then ad-lib around it.|A built-in control sample. The ad-read is what stiff dialogue sounds like. Everything around it is speech.|

## 3\. Why dialogue sounds written (or AI-made)

Beginners and AI models fail in the same direction: they try to be **clear, polite and complete**. Real speech is none of those things when it's emotional. Here are the tells to hunt for in your own drafts.

* **Everyone speaks in full, balanced sentences.** *Fix:* fragments, cut-offs, a word swapped mid-line.
* **Characters announce their emotions.** "I feel overwhelmed and sad." Real people rarely name it, and when they do, it comes out clumsy or sideways.
* **Turn-taking is too clean.** Question, complete answer, question, complete answer. Let answers drift, questions go ignored, two people talk at once.
* **Everyone is equally articulate and equally nice.** The teenager, the cop and the grandmother all sound like the same polite narrator.
* **Therapy-speak.** "I hear you." "That's valid." "I appreciate you sharing that." "Let's unpack that." Almost nobody talks like this at a kitchen table.
* **Exposition disguised as talk.** Characters telling each other things both already know, for the reader's benefit.
* **The tidy closer.** Each speech ends on a wise line, a neat list of three, or "It's not X, it's Y."
* **Speeches.** More than three sentences with no interruption, no action, no reaction. Break it up or cut it.
* **Constant agreement.** No friction, no misreading, no petty stuff. Real people disagree about small things when the real thing is too big.
* **Names in every line.** People rarely say each other's names mid-conversation unless they're angry, flirting, or calling someone over.

### Six more tells *(new)*

* **The ad-read voice.** Characters who talk like a sponsor read: "Here's the deal, and this part matters." Complete sentences, a setup, a benefit, a closing slogan. If it could be a pre-roll, it isn't dialogue.
* **Everyone is funny in the same rhythm.** Setup, punchline, tag, from every speaker. Real funny people differ: one deadpans, one escalates, one laughs before the end.
* **Perfect recall.** Nobody loses a name, a year, or the point. Real talk is full of "what was I going to say?" and "what year was that?"
* **The invisible room.** Talk floats in white space. In the transcripts, the room keeps intruding: a drink, a mic, a phone, a car, a baby. Give your characters something to hold and something to be interrupted by.
* **Confession on cue.** A character reveals something vulnerable exactly when the scene needs it. In real talk, disclosures slip out sideways, mid-joke, and the room moves on.
* **Fluent liars.** A character with a secret who tells a smooth, detailed, grammatical account. Liars either stonewall or over-furnish.

### Five more tells *(v3)*

* **Everyone wins the argument.** In real arguments nobody concedes. In the debate transcript, the closing show of hands produced "I understand it less." Let your characters leave less convinced than they arrived.
* **Strangers skip the small-talk ritual.** Fiction rushes to plot. Real strangers open with "How are you? You good?" and a name. Add the ritual, then let it fail.
* **Point-by-point rebuttals.** Real arguers answer the last thing said or the thing that stung. Nobody answers in order.
* **Characters who all sound alike.** If a reader needs a label to know who is talking, the voices are the same. Two similar-sounding characters need different dials, not name tags.
* **Ignoring the rules of the situation.** In a game, a meeting or an interview, some things can't be said. A scene that ignores its own constraints feels loose.

## 4\. The toolkit: twenty-six techniques

Techniques 1–9 are from the original guide. Techniques 10–18 were added in v2. Techniques 19–26 are new in v3.

### 1\. Write the want, not the feeling

Before any line, answer: **what does this character want right now?** To be left alone. To be forgiven. To change the subject. To get the other person to ask again. The line is a move toward that want, not a report on their inner state.

|Instead of|Try|
|-|-|
|"I'm hurt that you forgot my birthday."|"No, it's fine. Dave's mum texted me, actually. So."|

Specific, deflecting, trailing off. The reader supplies the hurt.

### 2\. Subtext: argue about the dishes

In the interview, the psychiatrist notes that couples who come in furious at each other are usually saying, underneath, *I want to be closer to you.* That's subtext in one sentence. Write the argument about the late text, the route, the rice, and let the reader hear the real thing underneath.

> "You're back late."
> "Traffic."
> "I made rice."
> "I ate at work."
> A pause. "Okay."

Surface: rice. Underneath: *I waited for you.*

### 3\. Let people miss each other

Real conversation is full of near-misses: answering the wrong question, assuming the wrong thing, hearing a different request. These small misses create tension without any villain.

> "Are you coming Saturday?"
> "What's Saturday?"
> "Mum's thing."
> "Did she say I had to?"

He never says yes or no. He asks about obligation, which is the real issue.

### 4\. Give every voice a fingerprint

Pick four dials per character and keep them steady:

* **Length:** clipped or rambling?
* **Hedging:** "I think, kind of, I mean" versus flat statements.
* **Humour:** teasing, deadpan, self-deprecating, or none.
* **Blind spot:** the topic they dodge or the word they never use.

In the couple's quiz, one partner hedges and qualifies ("I mean," "I think," "kind of"); the other rules answers in or out fast ("Boom."). The host runs warm and fast, stacking clauses and checking in on herself mid-sentence. The psychiatrist is slower and reaches for plain examples. **Test:** cover the names. Can you still tell who is talking?

*(new)* A **tic** is the cheapest fingerprint: "Can I defend myself real quick?" tells you who is speaking and what they fear. Give one character one tic. Don't give it to everyone.

### 5\. Specifics beat summaries

"I like talking to strangers" is a summary. A hotel elevator, seven electrical contractors, and a recommendation for the almond croissant is a person. One concrete, slightly odd detail does more emotional work than a paragraph of explanation. Ask of every line: *could anyone have said this, or only this character?*

### 6\. Interrupt, overlap, trail off

* Em dash for a cut-off: "I just thought—"
* Ellipsis, sparingly, for trailing or searching: "It's... I don't know the word."
* One-word lines for overlap and quick banter.
* Don't write "um" in every line. On a recording, "um" does a job. On the page, it's noise. Show hesitation through structure: restart the sentence, swap a word, stop early.

### 7\. Echo and callback

Let characters pick up each other's words, and let them share history. The couple jokes about how one always falls asleep first, and the next night they resume the film at the exact moment they left. Two friends who've had a phone call every Friday at noon for thirty years. These tell the reader these two have a past without a line of backstory. Use one callback per scene. *(Technique 10 below shows how to grow a callback into a full gag.)*

### 8\. Tease

Banter is how people who like each other fight, flirt and comfort. Keep it affectionate with a small cut in it. "Is that your other boyfriend?" says more about a relationship than "We have a loving partnership." *(new)* In the new transcripts, the **roast is the greeting**: the guest is mocked within seconds of sitting down. When the insult comes first, the warmth is already proven.

### 9\. Beats and silence instead of tags

Replace "she said sadly" with what she does: turning the jar over, wiping a clean counter, looking at the sink. And let a line sit alone on the page. White space is a pause. Don't put a beat after every line, or the rhythm turns mechanical.

> \*\*DON'T OVERCORRECT\*\*
> Adding mess isn't the same as adding truth. A character who stammers on every line is as artificial as one who never does. Mess must be motivated: nerves, avoidance, searching for a word, a feeling too big to say.

### 10\. Build a bit, then pay it off *(new)*

A running gag has three beats: **setup, escalation, payoff at the exit.** The Montreal joke in transcript B does exactly this. It starts as a throwaway, grows into mock-history, and returns as the sign-off.

> \*\*Beat 1:\*\* "You've got the lasagna face."
> \*\*Beat 2 (middle of the scene):\*\* "Don't make the lasagna face. I will not apologise to lasagna."
> \*\*Beat 3 (last line):\*\* She holds the door for him. "Lasagna face," she says, and he almost smiles.

Rules: the gag should be *theirs* (rooted in something that happened between them), it should change each time it appears, and the last use should carry feeling, not just a laugh.

### 11\. Yes-and: build together, let facts deflate it *(new)*

Close friends invent in turns. Each adds one detail and nobody says "that's untrue." The chain gets absurd, then a plain fact drops in and lands as a punchline.

> "He's got, what, six kids?"
> "And a tattoo of it."
> "Of the six kids."
> "Of the \*phrase.\* Right here."
> "Three," Richie said. "I have three."

(Based on the structure in transcript E; the lines are original.)

### 12\. Undercut the expected answer *(new)*

When a question invites a big, heroic answer, give a small, specific, slightly embarrassing one. "What did you do when you were bullied?" can be "I got a bodybuilder's physique," or it can be a pompadour. The second is a character.

### 13\. Commit to the absurd with a straight face *(new)*

Conan's own summary of what makes a funny performer, in transcript C, is committing to something absurd with incredible intensity. The deadpan claim that a drink gives superhuman strength works the same way. Write the character who believes it, not the character who winks.

### 14\. Give the scene a steward *(new)*

In every transcript, someone keeps trying to steer: "We got to start." "Let's go." "Back to the point." This character creates friction and rhythm, because everyone else keeps pulling away. If your scene is drifting pleasantly, add someone who wants to get to the point.

### 15\. Write liars with a flaw, not a flourish *(new)*

From the impostor game in transcript F: players distrust stories with **too many details**, and also stories with **none**. The honest player was just a little boring, a little inconsistent, and didn't try to sell.

|Lying|Telling the truth|
|-|-|
|Over-furnished: times, colours, names|Specific but uncertain: "I think it was Tuesday."|
|Answers the exact question, smoothly|Answers slightly sideways, hedges, backtracks|
|Adds one *unnecessary* detail to feel real|Includes a detail that doesn't help the story|
|Gets angry when doubted|Gets confused when doubted|

### 16\. Fish with questions *(new)*

A fake psychic doesn't state things; she asks things: "Did he drive a red car?" Each question offers a shape and waits for the client to fill it. Use this when you want one character to extract a confession, or when a con needs to feel real. The more the victim volunteers, the more the reader sees the trick.

### 17\. Patter: talk that covers the action *(new)*

The magician in transcript F chats about an Oreo and a deck of cards while the trick is done under cover of the chat. In fiction, let the real action happen under an unrelated conversation: a character steals a ring while asking about the weather; a doctor delivers news while discussing parking. The trivial talk is the misdirection, and the reader feels the real thing under it.

### 18\. Let someone lose the thread *(new)*

"What was I going to say?" is one of the most human lines in the transcripts. Use it for a reason: the character is nervous, tired, high, grieving, or avoiding the point. Let them recover with something truer than what they were going to say.

\---

### 19\. Name them with a want *(v3)*

A stage name or nickname does three jobs at once: it says who they are, what they want, and how they see themselves. A contestant who calls himself a name that begins "Little" and promises to be "Big" when he gets paid has told you the whole character in two words. Give a character a name they chose, and let someone ask where it came from.

### 20\. The refrain: a line that returns, and a bit that changes around it *(v3)*

In transcript G, each audition ends in the same dismissal. The line never changes; everything leading up to it does. Pick one phrase for a scene (or a relationship) and let it come back at the end of each beat. The reader starts to wait for it, which is rhythm.

### 21\. Fight about the conversation *(v3)*

In the debate transcript, the arguers keep fighting about the floor: "let me cut you off," "I'm not done," "why did you cut me off?" Add this to any argument. Heated speakers spend half their energy defending their right to speak. A moderator-type (see technique 14) makes it worse by being polite.

### 22\. Blanket claim against a single story *(v3)*

One character issues a rule about people. The other answers with one specific case. Neither moves. This is how two people talk past each other without either being stupid. Let the specific one be the more vulnerable.

### 23\. The small-talk loop, and its break *(v3)*

Strangers repeat the same formula all day ("How are you? You good? Where are you from?"). In fiction, give your characters the formula, then break it once. The moment someone does not ask, or does not answer, the scene has a hinge.

> "How are you? You good?"
> "Fine."
> "Where are you from?"
> "Is this the line for the second floor?"
> "...Yeah. It's this one."

### 24\. Constrain what can be said *(v3)*

In *Best in Jest*, the answerer can only say yes or no. Every answer is bare, so the guesser reads everything into timing and tone, and the answerer cheats with hedges. Use a rule (a game, a hearing, an NDA, a hospital rule, a promise not to tell) to force characters to speak around the thing. The rule is a ready-made source of subtext.

### 25\. Think out loud, with a system *(v3)*

Let a character narrow down options in speech: "Not blue, not purple. Not from a game. I have a system." You learn their method, their tics and their blind spots, and you hear them lose confidence. Don't summarise the thought; keep the process messy, and let one wrong turn recur.

### 26\. Formal procedure, casual people *(v3)*

Two moves from the transcripts are the same trick. A host reads cruel audience questions in a flat, official voice, and the guest isn't allowed to react. Two hosts adopt a theatrical, mock-solemn voice for an announcement and drop it a line later. The gap between register and content is the comedy; in a serious scene, it is the pressure (a doctor reading a form, a boss reading a policy).

## 5\. Writing emotion without announcing it

Strong feeling makes people **less** articulate, not more. Written dialogue often gets this backwards: the more intense the moment, the more eloquent the character becomes. In real life the opposite happens. Here is how emotion actually shows up in speech.

|How it leaks|What it sounds like|
|-|-|
|**Displacement**|Grief goes into logistics, errands, a jar of pickles, whether anyone has eaten.|
|**Over-politeness**|"That's fine. Thank you for letting me know." Formality is a wall.|
|**Sudden specificity**|A tiny irrelevant detail: "The second step was always loose. He never fixed it."|
|**Humour as armour, then a crack**|A joke lands, everyone laughs, then one line later it isn't funny anymore.|
|**Repetition**|Saying the same thing three ways because it isn't landing. "I'm fine. I said I'm fine."|
|**Shrinking lines**|Sentences get shorter as feeling climbs. The longest speech is often the least honest one.|
|**Reflex and deflection**|Deflecting a compliment, or blaming oneself, like the self-blame reflex in the interview.|
|**The small confession**|After a long, meandering exchange, one plain short line: "I wish I hadn't sent it." The contrast with the chatter is what lands.|
|**Surprise at oneself**|"I don't know why I'm crying." Characters are often the last to understand what they feel.|
|**The sincere turn** *(new)*|After a long run of teasing, one character says something straight. "Don't let them work you to death." Then, usually, a tiny joke to let everyone breathe.|
|**The soft landing** *(new)*|After a heavy line, the reply is short and warm ("Thank you, man. Of course."), not an analysis. The room then moves on. The joke that follows is a gift, not a retreat.|
|**The apology reflex** *(new)*|A character goes too far in a joke, hears it, and backs up: "I'm so sorry. That was rude." The retreat is more revealing than the joke.|
|**Care as nagging** *(new)*|"Did you eat today?" "Walk two miles a day." Love in the shape of logistics.|
|**The mortality joke** *(new)*|"You will die" said as a punchline, followed by a sincere "I'm glad I'm here." The joke carries what the character can't say directly.|
|**The unmatched reply** *(v3)*|A plain, vulnerable line is answered with a challenge or a change of subject, not a soft landing. The room's heat refuses it.|
|**The lost topic** *(v3)*|After a hard stretch, someone says they forgot what they were arguing about. The lull is where the real feeling surfaces.|
|**A plan instead of a hug** *(v3)*|"Let's go to the library." Comfort delivered as an errand.|
|**A joke that reframes forgetting** *(v3)*|"I lost my train of thought with sadness." The light line lets a feeling in without naming it.|

### Four structural moves

* **Don't start at ten.** Build. The first lines of a hard conversation are small talk, logistics, nothing. The reader feels the pressure under them.
* **Put the heaviest line next to the lightest.** A plain sentence after a run of jokes or chatter hits harder than any speech.
* **Give one line per scene permission to be direct.** If everything is subtext, nothing is. Save the plain, unguarded sentence for the moment it costs the character something.
* **Land softly after a disclosure** *(new)*. Let the other person answer in three words, not thirty. Resist the urge to have them process.

> \*\*WHY THE INTERVIEW WORKS\*\*
> The heaviest moments in it arrive in everyday phrasing: \*some mornings I wake up confused or sad; nobody on the planet has it all figured out.\* No ornament. Because it's said plainly, in the middle of ordinary talk, it feels true.

## 6\. Five scenes, two drafts each

### Scene 1: Clearing out Dad's kitchen

**Setup:** a brother and sister clearing out their late father's kitchen.

#### Draft A: stiff

> "I am feeling very overwhelmed by Dad's passing," said Mira. "I know that we both loved him deeply, and it is important that we allow each other to grieve."
>
> "I appreciate you saying that," Jun replied. "I have been struggling as well. I want you to know that I am here for you, and that together we will get through this."

**What's wrong:** both characters name their feelings; therapy-speak ("I appreciate you saying that"); perfectly symmetrical turns; no objects, no place, no interruption; the last line is a neat moral. Could be any two people anywhere.

#### Draft B: spoken

> Mira held up a jar of something pale and floating. "Keep or toss?"
>
> "What is it?"
>
> "Pickled... I don't know. Onions? He wrote the date on the lid."
>
> Jun took it from her and turned it over. "Twenty nineteen."
>
> "So, toss."
>
> "Yeah. No, yeah, toss it." He didn't put it down.
>
> "Jun."
>
> "I'm putting it down."
>
> "You're not."
>
> "I'm putting it— okay. I'm keeping the lid. Is that weird? I'm keeping the lid."
>
> "It's not weird."
>
> "It's got his handwriting on it."
>
> "I said it's not weird." She took the jar and kept her eyes on the sink. "Did you eat today? I'm not doing this on an empty stomach."

**What changed**

* No one says "grief." It's in the jar, the lid, the handwriting.
* Wants drive the lines. He wants to keep something without admitting it. She wants to protect him without being soppy.
* Echo and repetition: "I'm putting it down," "I'm keeping the lid," "It's not weird."
* A false start and a restart ("I'm putting it—") show him changing his mind in real time.
* Displacement: her last line swerves from the lid to food, which is how she says *I can't do this.*
* Specifics: 2019, the lid, the sink. Nobody could have said this but these two.
* Nobody delivers the moral. The scene stops mid-feeling.

### Scene 2: The wrong address *(new)*

**Setup:** three friends in a car, late to pick up a guest. One of them gave the guest the wrong address.

#### Draft A: stiff

> "Marcus, I am frustrated that you gave our guest the wrong address," said Priya.
>
> "I understand your frustration," Marcus replied. "I apologize for my mistake, and I will be more careful in the future. I value our friendship."
>
> "Thank you for apologizing. I forgive you."

**What's wrong:** feelings named, apology on a template, therapy-speak, a tidy resolution. No bit, no tease, no car.

#### Draft B: spoken

> "Forty minutes." Priya didn't look up from her phone. "He's standing outside a dentist's office in Reseda."
>
> "It was a typo."
>
> "It was a different zip code, Marcus."
>
> "One digit."
>
> "It was a different \*valley.\*"
>
> From the back seat Dev said, "Is the dentist at least nice?"
>
> "He's thinking about a crown, apparently." She put the phone face down on her knee. "He said it's fine. He said it so nicely. I hate it when they're nice about it."
>
> "I'll buy him lunch."
>

>

>
> Nobody said anything. A truck went by.
>
> "Left here," Priya said. "Not that left. The next one."

**What changed**

* The running gag (dentist, crown) escalates and returns.
* Priya's anger is about the guest's niceness, not the address. That's the actual hurt.
* Marcus's sincere line is small and comes after the jokes.
* Forgiveness is delivered as directions. Nobody says "I forgive you."
* Dev, from the back seat, is the tease. Every car scene needs one.

### Scene 3: The alibi *(new)*

**Setup:** a detective asks a woman where she was on Tuesday evening.

#### Draft A: the over-furnished lie

> "I left work at 6:42 exactly and drove my blue sedan home. There was a cyclist in a yellow vest on Alder Street. I stopped at the grocery and bought oat milk and a baguette. The cashier, Linda, was wearing a green scarf and told me about her cat, Mr. Whiskers."

**What's wrong:** times, colours, names, a pet. It's too complete, too confident, too chronological. Every detail is there to be believed.

#### Draft B: the dull truth

> "Home. I think? Tuesday's bin day, so I did the bins. Then I watched something. The one with the lawyers. Don't ask me which episode."
>
> "Anyone see you?"
>
> "The neighbour's dog. He barks at the bin." She almost laughed. "Sorry. That's not helpful, is it?"

**What changed**

* She hedges ("I think?"), answers sideways, and includes a detail that doesn't help her.
* The humour is accidental, which is more believable than polished.
* The apology reflex ("Sorry. That's not helpful") reads as a person, not a statement.
* The reader trusts Draft B more, even though it proves less.

## 7\. Revision checklist

Run every important dialogue scene through these passes. Do them in order.

1. **Read it aloud**, or have someone read it. Your mouth will catch every line that was written for the eye. Anywhere you stumble or feel embarrassed, rewrite.
2. **Cut the first line and the last line of every speech.** Most speeches start with throat-clearing and end with a moral.
3. **Delete every sentence that names a feeling.** Then see which ones you actually miss. Put back one at most.
4. **Strip names and exposition.** Remove names used mid-conversation and facts the characters both know.
5. **Find the want.** Write one sentence per character per scene: *she wants \_\_\_.* If you can't, the scene is idle talk.
6. **Cover the names.** Can you still tell who is speaking? If not, sharpen the four dials: length, hedging, humour, blind spot.
7. **Break up any speech.** Add an interruption, an action, a misunderstanding, or cut it by half.
8. **Add one of each.** One specific detail, one near-miss between the characters, one moment of teasing or warmth.
9. **Check the mess budget.** Every false start and filler must have a reason. If it's decoration, delete it.
10. **Check the endings.** End the scene earlier. Leave the last emotional beat unsaid.

### New passes *(new)*

11. **The ad-read test.** Highlight any line that sounds like a sponsor read: balanced clauses, a benefit, a tagline. Rewrite it as someone talking, or give it to a character who is *supposed* to sound like that.
12. **The gag audit.** If a joke or phrase appears twice, does it change? Does the last appearance carry feeling? If it never changes, cut it.
13. **The lie check.** For every character who is hiding something, count their details. Too many, or none? Add a boring one, a hedge, or an unneeded aside.
14. **The room check.** Where are the characters? Is there an object, a sound, an interruption? If the scene could happen in a white void, add one thing they hold and one thing that interrupts them.
15. **The sincere-line check.** Find the plain line. Is it surrounded by chatter or jokes? Is the reply short? Is there a soft landing, or does the next character give a speech?
16. **The tangent check.** If a character goes off on a tangent, does *someone* notice? A person in the scene should be aware.

### More passes *(v3)*

17. **The floor check.** In any argument, who has the floor? Add one interruption and one "I'm not done."
18. **The ritual check.** When strangers or service staff speak, do they use the short, procedural lines real people use? Add one polite formula, then let one character break it.
19. **The rules check.** If characters are in a game, a meeting, a hearing or an interview, what can't they say? Use that restriction.
20. **The reply check.** After the plainest line in the scene, does the next speaker meet it? If not, is that deliberate?
21. **The ending check.** Does anyone need to be convinced? Try ending with nobody convinced, and a small gesture (a promise to call, a changed seat) in place of agreement.
22. **The label check.** If two characters sound alike, separate them by the four dials (length, hedging, humour, blind spot). Don't just rely on tags.

> \*\*A FAST TEST\*\*
> Pick the most emotional line in your scene. Ask: would a real person say this out loud, to this person, right now, at this volume? If it sounds like a line from a movie trailer, it needs to be smaller.

## 8\. Sixteen practice drills

1. **Transcript to scene.** Record 60 seconds of a real conversation (a voice memo, a podcast clip, dinner). Write it out raw. Then rewrite it as fiction: keep three messy features, cut the rest, add one specific object.
2. **Five wants, one line.** Take "I'm fine." Write five versions where the speaker wants: to be asked again, to be left alone, to start a fight, to protect someone, to hide that they've been crying.
3. **The cover test.** Write twelve lines between two people with no tags. Cover the names. Can a friend tell who's who?
4. **The neighbouring question.** Write ten questions. In each reply, the character answers a nearby question instead. Keep the ones that reveal something.
5. **Cut it in half.** Take an emotional scene and cut every feeling-word and the last line of each speech. Read it aloud. Is it better? (It usually is.)
6. **Ad-read swap** *(new)*. Write a five-line product pitch in perfect sponsor voice. Then write the same moment as three friends ad-libbing around it, including one who teases the pitch.
7. **Run a gag** *(new)*. Invent one in-joke between two characters. Use it three times in one scene: setup, escalation, quiet payoff.
8. **True-and-false pair** *(new)*. Two characters answer the same question, one honestly and one lying, no tags. Can a friend tell which is which? Use the table in technique 15.
9. **Yes-and chain** *(new)*. Write five lines where each character adds one more absurd detail to a shared story. Make line six a flat, true fact from the person it's about.
10. **Joke, joke, joke, plain** *(new)*. Write a scene of five fast jokes ending with one plain line and a three-word reply. Don't explain it.
11. **Name game** *(v3)*. Invent five nicknames or stage names, each containing a want. Write one line of each person explaining where the name came from.
12. **Floor fight** *(v3)*. Write twelve lines of an argument where each line responds to the last three words of the line before.
13. **Small-talk loop** *(v3)*. Write a stranger exchange that uses the same formula three times and breaks on the fourth.
14. **Rule-bound confession** *(v3)*. Have a character reveal something using only "yes," "no" and "kind of," in a game, a hearing or an NDA.
15. **Think aloud** *(v3)*. Write 150 words of a character narrowing down options out loud. No summary. One wrong turn that returns.
16. **Formal reading** *(v3)*. One character reads a list of absurd, cruel or sad questions to another, who may not react. Write the reaction in beats only.

## Appendix A: Cleaning a raw transcript for fiction *(new)*

The six new transcripts are raw, machine-generated text, and they show why transcripts can't be pasted into fiction. Here's what to expect and what to do about it.

|What you'll see|What to do|
|-|-|
|`>>` speaker markers, with no names|Assign speakers by voice and tic. If you can't tell who's talking, don't use the line.|
|Words misheard or mangled ("Aban Costello")|Fix the phrase or replace it with something you'd actually hear.|
|Censored words and sound tags (`\[ \_\_ ]`, `\[music]`, `\[laughter]`)|Decide the word. Use a beat or a reaction instead of a sound tag.|
|Sponsor reads dropped into the middle of a conversation|Delete, or keep one as a deliberate contrast of written vs. spoken voice.|
|Repeated words ("I I I know")|Keep one restart, if it reveals nerves or thinking.|
|Long runs of overlap|Keep two or three fast lines. Summarise the rest in a beat.|
|Jokes that depend on a specific performer's timing|Steal the *structure* (setup, escalation, deflation), not the wording.|
|Timestamps, grouped or on every line *(v3)*|Strip them. Use them only to feel pace: a long gap is a beat.|
|The same conversation supplied twice *(v3)*|Check you aren't counting one conversation twice when you pick examples.|
|Auto-captions that merge speakers or mis-hear names *(v3)*|Re-listen. Don't trust speaker attribution, especially when two voices sound alike.|
|Lines that only make sense with an image or a game card *(v3)*|Replace with an action the reader can see.|

**Procedure**

1. Pick a 60-second stretch that surprised you.
2. Underline what the speakers *want* in that stretch (to be liked, to change the subject, to win).
3. Keep at most three features of the mess (a restart, a tic, a callback).
4. Add one concrete object and one thing in the room that interrupts.
5. Read it aloud. Cut anything that only makes sense with the audio.

## Appendix B: What comedy talk can't teach you *(new)*

These transcripts are conversations between **professional talkers** who are performing for each other, a microphone, and an audience. That's a gift and a trap.

* **They are faster and funnier than most people.** If every character in your story keeps up this pace, you've written a podcast. Give most characters slower, flatter, less quotable speech.
* **They are always "on."** The real intimacy is in the moments when the performance drops: the plain disclosure, the sincere turn, the "I'm glad I'm here." Look for those and write toward them.
* **They tease everything.** In a drama, a tease is a risk. One character who can't take a tease is a conflict.
* **They interrupt constantly.** On the page, one well-placed interruption does more than ten.
* **Their shared history is real.** In fiction, you have to *create* it with one callback, one nickname, one object, and then let the reader believe in the rest.
* **They are paid to be entertaining.** Your characters are paid by the story. Every line must also move the want, reveal the character, or raise the pressure.
* **Some of the new sources are arguments, and arguments are performances too.** The debate speakers talk for an audience as much as for each other. Your characters should argue because each wants something from the other.
* **Games and bits come with built-in rules.** A yes/no game or a refrain is easy to write because the structure does the work. Your scene needs its own structure, or its own rule.
* **Harsh content isn't the lesson.** Several sources contain cruelty used for laughs or for shock. Borrow the mechanics (interruption, escalation, deflection) and decide for yourself what your characters say.

Use these conversations as a tuning fork. Hear what's alive in them, then write something quieter.
